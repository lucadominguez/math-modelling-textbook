## Exponential Growth Model and Explicit Solutions

This section introduces the exponential growth model for a discrete time dynamical system. You will learn how to derive an explicit solution for a population growing at a constant relative rate and how to use that solution to answer questions about future population size and doubling time.

### Defining the Discrete Time Dynamical System

A discrete time dynamical system (also called a difference equation) describes how a quantity changes from one time step to the next. In this course, we focus on one dimensional difference equations, meaning the state space has only one variable.

We begin with a modeling problem: a population of yeast grows at 10 percent per hour. The initial population when the clock starts is 100,000. We check the population every hour, so our time steps are discrete: 0 hours, 1 hour, 2 hours, and so on. There is no assumption about what happens between these checkpoints.

### The Relative Growth Rate

In mathematical modeling of biological populations, we often use the relative growth rate. The relative growth rate `r(n)` is defined as the change in population divided by the current population.

### Deriving the Difference Equation

From the relative growth rate equation, we can derive the difference equation that describes how the population changes each hour.

Here is the step-by-step reasoning:

1.  Start with the definition: `ΔX(n) = X(n+1) - X(n)`.
2.  Substitute into the relative growth rate equation: `(X(n+1) - X(n)) / X(n) = 0.1`.
3.  Multiply both sides by `X(n)`: `X(n+1) - X(n) = 0.1 X(n)`.
4.  Add `X(n)` to both sides: `X(n+1) = X(n) + 0.1 X(n) = 1.1 X(n)`.

The resulting difference equation is `X(n+1) = 1.1 X(n)`. This is the stepping algorithm: to find the population at the next hour, multiply the current population by 1.1. The initial condition is `X(0) = 100,000`.

### Finding the Doubling Time

We can also ask: when does the population double? We want to find `n` such that `X(n) = 200,000`.

1.  Set up the equation: `(1.1)^n * 100,000 = 200,000`.
2.  Divide both sides by 100,000: `(1.1)^n = 2`.
3.  Solve for `n` using logarithms: `n = ln(2) / ln(1.1)`.
4.  Calculate the result: `n ≈ 0.6931 / 0.0953 ≈ 7.27`.

The exact mathematical answer is approximately 7.27 hours. However, because we are working with a discrete time system where we only check at whole hours, we must consider the discrete nature of the process. At hour 7, the population has not yet doubled. At hour 8, the population will be more than double the initial amount. So in practice, we would observe the doubling at the 8th hour.

If this were a continuous time system, we could simply say 7.27 hours. But the discrete iterative hopping process means we only have data at integer time steps.

### Why Explicit Solutions Matter

The yeast population example is simple because we can solve it explicitly. Many discrete dynamical systems are not so simple. When explicit solutions are impossible, we need other methods to understand the system's behavior. This is where graphical methods, such as cobweb diagrams, become valuable. They provide insight into the dynamics even when we cannot write a closed-form formula.

## Logistic Growth Model with Carrying Capacity

The exponential growth model from the previous section assumes a population can grow without limit. This is unrealistic for any real biological system. In this section, you will modify the exponential growth model to include a carrying capacity, which represents the maximum population size that the environment can sustain.

### The Problem with Unlimited Growth

The exponential model `x(n+1) = 1.1 * x(n)` predicts that a yeast population starting at 100,000 will grow to 146,410 after 4 hours and double in approximately 7.27 hours. However, this model implies that the population could eventually reach a million, a billion, or even a trillion. In reality, a petri dish has finite space and resources. The yeast will eventually run out of room and compete for what is available.

The key flaw in the exponential model is the assumption of a constant growth rate. To fix this, you will make the growth rate depend on the current population size. The idea is simple: as the population increases, the growth rate should decrease.

### How the Growth Rate Changes with Population

The term `(1 - X(n) / K)` is the key to the model. It acts as a feedback mechanism.

- **When the population is far below the carrying capacity:** `X(n)` is much smaller than `K`, so `X(n) / K` is close to 0. The term `(1 - 0)` is close to 1. The growth rate is approximately `r`, which is the maximum rate. The population grows quickly.

- **When the population is at the carrying capacity:** `X(n)` equals `K`, so `X(n) / K` equals 1. The term `(1 - 1)` equals 0. The growth rate becomes 0. The population stops growing.

- **When the population exceeds the carrying capacity:** `X(n)` is greater than `K`, so `X(n) / K` is greater than 1. The term `(1 - something > 1)` becomes negative. The growth rate becomes negative. The population will decrease.

This behavior is realistic. When there are few yeast, they have plenty of room and resources, so they reproduce rapidly. As the population grows, competition for space and food increases, slowing reproduction. If the population becomes too large, there are not enough resources for everyone, and the population begins to die off.

### The Logistic Growth Equation

You can now derive the full discrete logistic growth model. Start with the definition of the change in population:

`ΔX(n) = X(n+1) - X(n)`

Substitute the new growth rate equation:

`ΔX(n) = r * X(n) * (1 - X(n) / K)`

Now solve for `X(n+1)`:

`X(n+1) = X(n) + r * X(n) * (1 - X(n) / K)`

This is the logistic growth model for discrete dynamical systems. It is a quadratic function because it contains an `X(n)^2` term (from multiplying `r * X(n) * (-X(n) / K)`).

## Oscillatory Convergence with Larger Growth Rate

### The Oscillatory Convergence

When the population overshoots K, the crowding effect becomes very strong. The population must contract. The next step brings the population back down, but it may overshoot again in the opposite direction. This creates a pattern of shooting up and pulling back.

This is analogous to what happens in a linear system when the multiplier (lambda) is between 1 and 2. ## Chaotic Dynamics for Very Large Growth Rate

In this section you will see what happens when the growth rate `R` becomes very large. The discrete logistic model is:

```
X(n+1) = X(n) + R * X(n) * (1 - X(n)/K)
```

where `K` is the carrying capacity. For simplicity, set `K = 1`. The function `F(x) = x + R * x * (1 - x/K)` is plotted on a cobweb diagram. The diagonal line is `y = x`. The cobweb diagram traces the orbit: start at `x0` on the x-axis, go vertically to `F(x0)`, then horizontally to the diagonal, then vertically to `F` again, and so on.

### Increasing R to Very Large Values

When `R` is small (e.g., `R < 2`), the orbit converges monotonically to the carrying capacity. **Steps to construct the cobweb for very large `R`:**

1. Start with a very small population `x0` (e.g., near 0).
2. Compute `F(x0)`. Because `R` is huge, `F(x0)` is a massive jump upward. The population grows enormously in one step.
3. From `F(x0)`, move horizontally to the diagonal line `y = x`. This gives the next input `x1 = F(x0)`.
4. From `x1`, go vertically to `F(x1)`. Since `x1` is very large (above carrying capacity), `F(x1)` is a huge drop downward. The population crashes.
5. From `F(x1)`, move horizontally to the diagonal. This new `x2` is very small again.
6. Repeat: the orbit shoots up, then crashes down, then shoots up again. Each time it overshoots the equilibrium.

### The Result: Chaos

For very large `R`, the orbit never settles into a fixed point or a periodic cycle. It continues to hop back and forth in a seemingly random way. The cobweb diagram becomes dense with lines; if you kept drawing, the diagram would fill in completely. This behavior is called **chaos**.

**Chaos** in a discrete dynamical system means:
- The orbit is deterministic (the equation is fixed).
- The orbit is aperiodic (it never repeats exactly).
- The orbit is sensitive to initial conditions (tiny changes in `x0` lead to wildly different future values).

The speaker notes that for `R` around `4` or `6` (with `K = 1`), the system is chaotic. (Added context: In the classic logistic map `x_{n+1} = r x_n (1 - x_n)`, chaos appears for `r` near `3.57` and above. The speaker’s `R` corresponds to `r` in that map when `K=1`.)

## Real Data and Conclusion

In this final section, you will see what happens when the growth rate becomes very large, learn about the real-world data that inspired the logistic growth model, and get specific parameter values to test on your own.

### Chaotic Behavior at High Growth Rates

When the growth rate R is very large, the population no longer settles into stable equilibrium points. Instead, the system becomes chaotic. The population values jump unpredictably between high and low numbers, sometimes above the carrying capacity and sometimes below it. This happens because the growth rate is so high that each time step produces enormous jumps in population size.


![This frame shows mathematical equations and graphs related to population dynamics, including an example of exponential growth and a logistic...](frames/frame_21_1720s.jpg)
*[28:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1hCX5Gbeo0E&t=1720s) This frame shows mathematical equations and graphs related to population dynamics, including an example of exponential growth and a logistic growth model.*


The whiteboard shows the original exponential growth model and the logistic growth model. The exponential model assumes a constant per capita growth rate of 10 percent per hour:

```
Ex: r(n) = ΔX(n)/X(n) = 0.1
=> ΔX(n) = 0.1 X(n)
=> X(n+1) = 1.1 X(n)
X(0) = 100,000
=> X(n) = (1.1)^n * 100,000
After 4 hours: X(4) = 146,410
When does pop. double?
Solve X(n) = 200,000
=> (1.1)^n = 2
=> n = ln(2)/ln(1.1) ≈ 7.27
```

The logistic growth model adds a carrying capacity term:

```
Ex: ΔX(n)/X(n) = r(1 - X(n)/K)
=> X(n+1) = X(n) + rX(n)(1 - X(n)/K)
F(x)
```

At high R values, the population does not get absorbed into equilibrium points. Instead, it wanders randomly between large and small populations, both above and below the carrying capacity. This seemingly random behavior is called chaos in discrete dynamical systems.

### Historical Data from Carlson (1913)

In 1913, a German researcher named Carlson collected real data on yeast populations. This data was the original inspiration for the simple exponential growth model shown earlier. The 10 percent growth rate per hour came directly from Carlson's measurements.

When you fit Carlson's actual data to a logistic growth model, you get specific parameter values. The iteration scheme becomes:

```
X(n+1) = 1.56X(n) - 0.00086X(n)^2
```

From this equation, you can extract the growth rate R and carrying capacity K:

| Parameter | Value | Meaning |
|-----------|-------|---------|
